Handstand

Adho Mukha Vrksasana·अधो मुख वृक्षासन·adho mukha vṛkṣāsana

Also called: Downward-Facing Tree Pose

Handstand is the asana that most students aspire to and most never achieve solo. The wall practice can become a stable medium-term home — many practitioners enjoy a 15-second wall handstand without ever attempting free balance. Both are legitimate.

Benefits

  • Most demanding inversion in the catalog — full-body strength and balance
  • Builds wrist, shoulder, and core conditioning unlike any other pose
  • The big inversion: cardiovascular, lymphatic, and confidence effects
  • Capstone pose for a strong asana practice

How to practise Handstand

  1. PREREQUISITE: strong Down Dog, plank, and shoulder stability. Practice at a wall.
  2. Place the palms on the floor about 6 inches from a wall, shoulder-width apart.
  3. Walk the feet toward the hands until the hips stack over the shoulders.
  4. One leg kicks up; the other follows. Heels touch the wall lightly.
  5. Stack: wrists → shoulders → hips → heels in one vertical line.
  6. Engage the core, press through the palms, gaze between the hands.
  7. To exit: lower one leg at a time, slowly back to Down Dog.

Suggested hold: 15s · Breath: Natural

Who should avoid or modify this pose

  • ADVANCED — practice with a teacher
  • Don't practice with wrist, elbow, shoulder, or neck injury
  • Skip with carpal tunnel syndrome
  • Pregnant women shouldn't practice
  • Avoid with high blood pressure, glaucoma, retinal detachment, or vertigo
  • Skip during menstruation if heavy inversions feel uncomfortable

If you're pregnant, injured, or managing a condition, check with a qualified teacher or clinician before practising.

Modifications

  • Practice at a wall — never solo in the middle of the room before the inversion is stable
  • Hold for 3-5 seconds at first, building over weeks
  • Build the prerequisites: Pincha Mayurasana (forearm stand), strong handstand at the wall
  • Use a spotter for the first many attempts

Traditional source: Modern hatha — Modern advanced inversion. Codified in 20th-century practice; not in HYP Ch. 1.
