Tortoise Pose

Kurmasana·कूर्मासन·kūrmāsana

Also called: Tortoise

Kurmasana is one of those poses where the geometry teaches you what's possible only after years of patient hamstring work. For most users it's aspirational; for those with the mobility, it's one of the most quietly transformative shapes in the catalog — deep withdrawal as practice.

Benefits

  • Deep release through the entire posterior chain (back, hamstrings, calves)
  • Hip-opener through external rotation under load
  • Calms the nervous system — the inward fold is contemplative
  • Connects modern practitioners to one of HYP's foundational shapes

How to practise Tortoise Pose

  1. Sit with the legs extended in a wide V.
  2. Bend the knees and place the feet flat on the floor about a leg-length apart, knees pointing up.
  3. Lean forward and thread each arm UNDER the corresponding bent knee.
  4. Walk the hands out to the sides under the thighs until the shoulders sit beneath the knees.
  5. Slowly straighten the legs as the torso lowers toward the floor.
  6. Rest the forehead or chin on the floor; the chest reaches toward the floor.
  7. Hold for several breaths. To exit: bend the knees, walk the hands back, sit up.

Suggested hold: 60s · Breath: Paced

Who should avoid or modify this pose

  • ADVANCED pose — work with a teacher before attempting solo
  • Don't practice with herniated disc, acute lower-back injury, or severe sciatica
  • Skip with knee or hip injury
  • Pregnant women shouldn't practice — the compression is unsafe
  • Avoid after recent abdominal surgery

If you're pregnant, injured, or managing a condition, check with a qualified teacher or clinician before practising.

Modifications

  • Practice Janu Sirsasana or Paschimottanasana first to build the hamstring length needed
  • Use a folded blanket under the hips and a bolster on the legs for restorative variant
  • Keep the knees bent — straightening them is the advanced expression
  • Build duration gradually; start with 15-second holds

Traditional source: Hatha Yoga Pradipika 1.22 — Named in HYP Ch. 1 alongside the other foundational asanas. Kurma is also the name of Vishnu's second incarnation as a tortoise — the pose evokes a contemplative withdrawal.
