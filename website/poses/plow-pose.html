Plow Pose

Halasana·हलासन·halāsana

Also called: Plough Pose

Halasana looks dramatic but is actually a quiet pose when the neck is protected with blankets and the feet are supported on a chair. For users with chronic tension between the shoulder blades from desk work, the upper-back release here is unmatched. Save the unsupported version for users who can manage Sarvangasana safely first.

Benefits

  • Stretches the entire posterior chain — hamstrings, calves, lower back, upper back, neck
  • Calms the nervous system through inversion + spinal flexion
  • Stimulates the thyroid through the throat compression
  • Traditionally taught as a sleep-inducing pose in long-hold restorative practice

How to practise Plow Pose

  1. Lie on your back. Bring the arms by your sides, palms down.
  2. Press the hands and arms into the floor as you lift the legs straight up toward the ceiling.
  3. Continue lifting and bring the legs over the head, lowering the toes toward the floor behind you.
  4. Support the lower back with the hands, fingers pointing toward the spine.
  5. If the toes don't reach the floor, place a chair or stack of blocks behind your head to rest the feet on.
  6. Keep the chin slightly tucked but DO NOT turn the head — neck stays neutral.
  7. To exit: support the lower back with the hands and slowly roll down vertebra by vertebra.

Suggested hold: 45s · Breath: Natural

Who should avoid or modify this pose

  • Don't practice with neck injury, herniated disc, or recent neck surgery
  • Pregnant women shouldn't practice — the depth of spinal flexion isn't safe
  • Skip during menstruation if you find inversions uncomfortable (cultural / personal)
  • Avoid with high blood pressure, glaucoma, or recent retinal detachment
  • Skip with severe asthma or hiatal hernia
  • Don't practice on a full stomach — wait 3-4 hours after eating

If you're pregnant, injured, or managing a condition, check with a qualified teacher or clinician before practising.

Modifications

  • Place a chair behind your head and rest the feet on the chair seat — the safer default for most users
  • Stack folded blankets under the shoulders so the neck doesn't bear weight (protects the cervical spine)
  • Practice Legs Up the Wall instead if Halasana feels inaccessible
  • Skip and substitute Setu Bandha Sarvangasana for similar nervous-system effect without the neck demand

Traditional source: Modern hatha 3.69 — Modern asana with lineage in the Hatha Yoga Pradipika 3.69 Viparita Karani mudra family. The Sanskrit 'hala' means plow — the body resembles the curve of an ox-drawn plow.
